How to Inject

The basic routine for a shot just under the skin.

  1. Wash your hands and lay out fresh supplies on a clean surface.
  2. Wipe the vial top with an alcohol pad and let it dry.
  3. Use a new syringe every time. Never reuse or share one.
  4. Pick a spot: belly (two inches from the belly button), thigh, or back of the arm.
  5. Wipe the spot with a fresh pad and let it air dry.
  6. Gently pinch the skin, inject slowly, then press lightly with a clean pad.
  7. Drop the syringe straight into your needle disposal box.
  8. Switch spots each time so one area doesn't get sore.

If the spot gets hot, swollen or oozing, or you have trouble breathing or your face or throat swells, get emergency help right away.

Pep Research

What I've learned about peptides, the three I use, and how to tell a good vial from a sketchy one.

So what is a peptide?

A peptide is a short chain of amino acids, the same building blocks that make up protein. Your body makes lots of them on its own. Some famous medicines are peptides too, like insulin and the GLP-1 shots behind Ozempic and Zepbound.

The three I use

Weekly

Retatrutide

Made by Eli Lilly. It's called a "triple agonist" because it works on three hunger and metabolism hormones at once: GIP, GLP-1 and glucagon.

In two big late-stage trials reported in 2026, people on the higher doses lost about 19–23% of their body weight over 80 weeks. Upset stomach, nausea and diarrhea were the most common side effects.

Still not approved. Lilly plans to apply to the FDA in early 2027.

Weekly

Cagrilintide

Made by Novo Nordisk. It copies amylin, a hormone that helps you feel full and slows how fast food leaves your stomach.

It's been studied on its own and paired with semaglutide in a combo called CagriSema.

Not approved as a medicine on its own.

Six days a week

KLOW

A blend, not a single peptide. It's usually sold as a mix of BPC-157, TB-500, GHK-Cu and KPV, but the recipe can vary from seller to seller.

Most of the research on those four is in animals or in the lab, and the blend itself hasn't been through big human trials.

Not FDA-approved.

Fine print, decoded

What "research use only" really means

That label is the shop telling you the product is meant for lab work. It isn't a prescription, it isn't a medicine that's been cleared for people, and it doesn't give anyone the green light to inject it. Plenty of popular peps are sold this way, including:

RetatrutideBPC-157TB-500CJC-1295GHK-Cu (injectable)

How I check a COA

A COA (Certificate of Analysis) is the lab report for one batch. Here's what I look for:

  1. Batch number matches. The lot number on the report should match the one on your vial.
  2. Purity percent. Usually tested with HPLC. Higher is better, and most good shops show 98% or more.
  3. Identity test. A mass spec result confirms the peptide is what the label says it is.
  4. A real lab. Look for a third-party lab's name and a recent test date, not just the shop's own logo.

Even a perfect COA only tells you what's in that one vial. It doesn't mean the product is approved or safe for people.

Storing your vials

  • Powder vials like it cool, dark and dry. Many people keep them in the fridge.
  • Once a vial is mixed, keep it in the fridge and go by the product's guidance for how long it lasts.
  • Keep vials out of hot cars, sunny windows and bathrooms.
  • Write the mix date on the vial so you never have to guess.

Pep dictionary

Tap a word to open it.

Lyophilized

Fancy word for freeze-dried. That's why your vial shows up as a little cake of powder. It keeps the peptide stable until you mix it.

Reconstitute

To turn that powder back into a liquid by adding a fluid. The right fluid and amount come from the specific product's instructions.

Subcutaneous (SubQ)

Into the fatty layer just under your skin, not into a muscle or vein.

Agonist

Something that switches on a receptor in your body. Retatrutide switches on three different ones.

Half-life

How long it takes your body to clear half of something. A longer half-life is why some peps are once a week instead of daily.

Weight vs. liquid

mgmilligrams of peptide
mcg1,000 in every mg
mLhow much liquid

Think of it like sweet tea. Two cups can be filled to the same line and still have totally different amounts of sugar, depending on how the batch was made.

Peps work the same way. How much peptide is in each mL depends on how the vial was mixed, so the line on your syringe alone can't tell you your dose. You have to know your vial's exact strength first.

Approved or not? Myth vs. fact

Myth

If a peptide has been researched, it's approved.

Fact

Approval is for one exact product, made one exact way, for one use. A study on a peptide doesn't approve every vial that shares its name.

Myth

FDA-approved means zero risk.

Fact

It means the agency decided the benefits outweigh the risks for certain people. Approved meds still have side effects and aren't right for everyone.

Myth

Not approved means it's dangerous.

Fact

Not always. It might still be in trials, the evidence might not be there yet, or the maker never applied. It just means nobody has officially confirmed it's safe, works, and is made right.

Myth

Getting approved is just paperwork.

Fact

The maker has to show safety, results, side effects, how it's manufactured, and exactly what it's for, usually across years of trials.

Other Popular Peps

The names you'll see everywhere, what people use them for, and which ones are actually approved.

GLP-1 · Novo Nordisk

Semaglutide

Copies a gut hormone that turns down hunger and steadies blood sugar. Sold as Ozempic for type 2 diabetes and Wegovy for weight loss.

Approved as Ozempic & Wegovy
GIP + GLP-1 · Eli Lilly

Tirzepatide

Works on two hormones at once, which is why it's often called a “dual agonist.” Reta's older sibling.

Approved as Mounjaro & Zepbound
Growth hormone signal

Tesamorelin

Nudges your body to release its own growth hormone. Prescribed to reduce belly fat in people with HIV-related fat buildup.

Approved as Egrifta
Bremelanotide

PT-141

Acts on the brain's melanocortin system. Prescribed for low sexual desire in women before menopause.

Approved as Vyleesi
Healing & recovery

BPC-157

Based on a protein found in stomach juices. Hugely popular for gut and injury recovery, but most of the research is in animals.

Not FDA-approved
Recovery

TB-500

A lab-made piece of thymosin beta-4, a protein involved in tissue repair. Mostly animal research, and banned in pro sports.

Not FDA-approved
Copper peptide

GHK-Cu

Found naturally in the body and loved in skincare serums for skin and hair. The injectable version isn't approved.

Not FDA-approved
Growth hormone pair

CJC-1295 + Ipamorelin

Often stacked together to prompt your own growth hormone release. Banned in pro sports.

Not FDA-approved
Metabolism

MOTS-c

Comes from the mitochondria, your cells' power plants. Studied for energy and metabolism, mostly in animals so far.

Not FDA-approved
Focus & calm

Semax & Selank

Nasal-spray peptides developed in Russia, where they're used for focus and anxiety. Not approved in the US.

Not FDA-approved
Anti-aging

Epitalon

Marketed for longevity and sleep. The research is small and mostly older studies.

Not FDA-approved
